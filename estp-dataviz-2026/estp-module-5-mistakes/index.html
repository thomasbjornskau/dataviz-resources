What is wrong with this chart?

The data is correct. The impression is not neutral.

Start with a plausible chart of quarterly unemployment. The points come directly from Statistics Norway. Only the framing is deliberately problematic.

Teaching example · SSB table 08518

Unemployment has moved sharply

A narrow vertical scale makes ordinary variation occupy most of the plotting area.

The problem

A narrow y-axis gives a small number of percentage points a large visual height.

Why it matters

The reader may interpret the movement as more consequential than the values justify.

The fix

Use a scale that preserves visible variation without making the magnitude look larger than it is.

Not a rigid rule: line charts do not always need to start at zero. Zero is especially important when length carries magnitude, as in bars. For lines, choose a scale that fits the analytical task and makes the magnitude interpretable.

Takeaway 1

A chart can be technically correct and still be misleading.

Scale choices, aggregation and visual emphasis can strongly affect interpretation even when every plotted value is correct.

Context changes the conclusion

The frame is part of the evidence.

Two choices that often look like formatting decisions — time horizon and denominator — can change what a reasonable reader concludes.

Why it matters

A short interval can make a movement look exceptional. A longer interval may show that comparable movements have happened before.

The fix

Choose the time horizon and measure that match the claim. Longer is not automatically better, and a rate is not universally better than a count.

Takeaway 3

Context is part of accuracy.

Baselines, denominators, time frames and comparison groups are not decorative additions. They can determine whether a correct number is interpreted correctly.

Raw, adjusted or trend?

Are these values actually comparable?

Monthly labour-market observations contain seasonal patterns and sampling variation. SSB table 13760 publishes unadjusted, seasonally adjusted, three-month moving-average and trend series.

Never switch adjustment status silently. SSB notes that seasonally adjusted and unadjusted monthly LFS figures are volatile and recommends trend and/or the three-month moving average for many uses.

Takeaway 2

Too much information is also a form of distortion.

Clutter, excessive categories and competing messages make the relevant pattern harder to see. Showing everything is not necessarily more neutral.

Visual emphasis is an argument

Colour creates meaning, whether you intend it or not.

These bars use the same 2026Q2 unemployment rates. Only the emphasis changes.

The accent currently makes 55–74 years look like the story.But the question is: which age group has the highest unemployment rate?

Takeaway 4

Colour and visual encoding can create false importance.

Strong colour, area and contrast should correspond to analytical importance, not merely to what the software selected first.

How certain is this number?

A precise-looking point is still an estimate.

The Labour Force Survey is sample-based. Absolute figures and rates estimate the population; they are not exact counts of every person in Norway.

Uncertainty visible · conceptual

Point estimate + plausible uncertainty

No numerical interval is claimed here. This schematic is deliberately separate from the 4.5% SSB value because the selected table extract does not provide an exact confidence interval to plot.

Uncertainty does not make statistics useless. It tells us how precisely they should be interpreted. The August 2026 LFS release also gives a concrete revision example: SSB revised the June 2026 trend unemployment rate from 4.7% to 4.5% when the July information was incorporated.

Takeaway 5

Uncertainty should not disappear in the visualization.

Sampling variation, revisions, breaks in series and methodological limitations matter whenever they can change the interpretation.

Breaks in time series

A connected line can imply comparability that metadata does not support.

The Norwegian LFS was substantially redesigned in 2021. SSB estimates a break of about 5,400 unemployed persons; that particular break estimate is not statistically significant.

Why it matters

A line joins observations visually. Readers may assume that the measurement process was unchanged unless told otherwise.

The fix

Keep the chart readable, but mark relevant breaks and put the methodological note close enough to affect interpretation.

Final takeaway

A trustworthy visualization does more than show correct numbers. It helps the reader form a proportionate interpretation of them.

A design decision becomes a problem when it makes the intended comparison harder or encourages an interpretation that the evidence does not support.

Accessibility is part of correctness: a pattern that some readers cannot perceive or operate is a communication failure, even when the underlying chart is statistically sound.
