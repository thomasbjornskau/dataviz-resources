Part 1 · the heart of it

Nesting, stacking — and the parentheses that bind them

Two moves decide everything: nesting places one variable inside another, while stacking sets them side by side as independent distributions. With three variables you can mix them — and then you need grouping to say what belongs together. This is where most people lose their footing.

In closing

Grammar, not guesswork

Composite tables look complicated, but they're built from a few simple moves: nesting places something inside something, stacking sets things side by side, grouping decides what belongs together, and totals can be placed at any level you like. And however you combine the variables, the whole arrangement can sit in the rows or in the columns — the axis is a choice, not a property of the figures. Once you recognise the moves, you can read any table by asking: what is nested inside what, what is stacked, where are the groupings and totals, and what's in rows versus columns? Tools like flextab write exactly this as a short expression — but the moves are the same whether you click or type.
