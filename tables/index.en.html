Part 1

Two dimensions: what runs down, and what runs across

A cross-table splits the same people two ways at once. What varies down the page is the row dimension; what varies across is the column dimension. Swap them and you'll see the content is identical — just turned on its side.

Part 2

Nesting and stacking: two ways to fit in more

To show another variable, you have two choices. Nesting places the new one inside the old — each age group is broken down by sex. Stacking sets the new one alongside as an independent block. The difference decides whether the totals mean anything.

Part 3

Totals are answers, not decoration

A total — what statisticians call a marginal — is the sum along the edge of the table. The row total answers "how many in this row altogether?", the column total does the same going down, and the bottom-right corner is everyone combined. Switch them on and see where each belongs.

Controls

The bottom-right corner appears when both are on — it's the grand total, and it has to match both ways.

Part 4 · the heart of it

Three percentages, three entirely different questions

This is where most people trip up. The same cell can be turned into a percentage three ways, and they don't mean the same thing. Pick a type below — the table shows which direction the percentage runs, and what adds up to 100.

Column percent

Of everyone in the column

"Among women — what share are aged 30–44?" Each column adds up to 100.

Row percent

Of everyone in the row

"Among those aged 30–44 — what share are women?" Each row adds up to 100.

Total percent

Of everyone combined

"What share of the whole population are women aged 30–44?" The whole table adds up to 100.

In closing

Read the direction before you read the number

Next time you meet a percentage table: first work out what adds up to 100. That tells you which question the author asked — and whether it's the same question you were wondering about. A well-labelled table says it outright. A poorly labelled one lets you guess, and people tend to guess wrong.
