Module 1 — In-depth review of different chart types

Choosing the right chart

There is no universally best chart. The useful question is which visual structure makes the statistical relationship easiest to see.

Everything on this page is drawn from one table: Statistics Norway, StatBank table 06913, population 1 January and population changes during the calendar year, 1951–2026. One table, six questions, six different visual structures.

What this module is for

Five things worth taking away

This is not a tour of a chart catalogue. It is a way of reasoning that still works when you meet a chart type you have never seen, or when you need one that does not have a name yet.

  1. Takeaway one

    Start with the analytical task, not the chart type

    Before a chart can be chosen, the job has to be named. Comparing values, showing change over time, describing a distribution, testing a relationship, breaking a total into parts and tracing a flow are six different jobs, and they do not have the same answer. Most weak charts in official statistics are not badly drawn. They are drawn well, and they answer a question the reader did not ask.

    CompareWhich is larger?
    ChangeWhere is this going?
    DistributionWhat is typical, what is extreme?
    RelationshipDo these move together?
    CompositionWhat is this total made of?
    FlowWhat went in, what came out?

  2. Takeaway two — the centre of this module

    Chart types are combinations of visual encodings

    A bar chart is not a primitive. It is position along a common scale, plus length from a shared baseline. A line chart is position plus connection. A heatmap is position twice over, plus colour. Position, length, area, colour, connection and direction are the building blocks, and every named chart is an arrangement of a few of them.

    This matters practically. If you memorise chart types you can only choose from the ones you remember. If you can read the encodings, you can reason about a chart you have never met, explain to a colleague exactly why one option is harder to read than another, and design something new when the standard forms do not fit.

    The interactive encoding lens further down highlights each encoding wherever it appears on this page.

  3. Takeaway three

    Use the simplest chart that preserves the insight

    Bars and lines are powerful because nobody has to learn them. The reader spends their attention on the statistics rather than on the graphic. That is a real budget, and specialised charts spend it.

    So a more elaborate chart has to earn its complexity: it should show something the simple form genuinely cannot. If a treemap and a sorted bar chart tell the reader the same thing, the bar chart wins.

  4. Takeaway four

    Specialised charts reveal structures classic charts cannot

    Sometimes the structure in the data is not a list of values. Treemaps show hierarchy and part-of-whole at the same time. Sankey diagrams show flow, direction and conservation — what came in, what went out, what remained. Heatmaps show patterns across two dimensions at once. Dot plots compress many comparisons into little space.

    The test is structural, not aesthetic. A Sankey is right when the data really is a flow that balances. On a table with no hierarchy, a treemap is decoration.

  5. Takeaway five

    The best chart is the one that makes the relevant comparison easy

    A technically correct chart can still be a poor choice. If the reader has to hold two numbers in their head, follow a line across a legend, or judge two areas against each other, the comparison that matters has been made expensive.

    Put the comparison you care about closest to the encoding people read most accurately, which is position along a common scale. Everything else in chart choice is downstream of that.

Reading the catalogue backwards

From encoding to chart

Written as recipes rather than definitions. The value of this list is not the seven rows; it is that you can extend it yourself, and that you can look at an unfamiliar chart and work out what it is made of.

Common chart forms and the visual encodings they are built from
Chart formBuilt fromGood when
Bar chart position + length Comparing magnitudes that share a zero baseline.
Line chart position + connection Change along an ordered variable, usually time.
Dot plot position (+ colour for series) Many comparisons in little space; no zero baseline required.
Scatter plot position × position Relationship between two quantities measured on the same units.
Heatmap position × position + colour Pattern across two dimensions where exact values matter less than shape.
Treemap area + containment (hierarchy) Part-of-whole with nesting, and too many categories for bars.
Sankey diagram connection + width + direction Flows that balance: everything that enters has to leave or remain.

Why there is no treemap on this page

Table 06913 is a flat annual time series for one region. It has no hierarchy, so a treemap would encode the components of population change as areas without adding a single thing a sorted bar chart does not already say — and it would make negative values impossible to draw. The flow view further up earns its complexity because the demographic accounting really does balance. A treemap here would not.

If you want a legitimate treemap from Statistics Norway, use a table with genuine nesting, such as population by county and municipality (StatBank table 07459), where the containment is real.

Do not ask “Which chart should I use?” until you have asked “What comparison should the reader be able to make?”

Data, definitions and transformations

Source

Statistics Norway, StatBank table 06913: Population 1 January and population changes during the calendar year, whole country (region code 0), 1951–2026. Table 06913 in StatBank.

Retrieved with a single HTTP GET from the PxWebApi v2 endpoint:

Units and definitions

  • Population — persons registered as resident in Norway on 1 January. A stock, measured at an instant.
  • Births, deaths, immigration, emigration — persons, counted over the calendar year. Flows, measured over a period.
  • Excess of births — births minus deaths. Negative means more deaths than births.
  • Net migration — immigration minus emigration. Negative means net outward migration.
  • Population growth — from 2005 onwards, the population on 1 January of the following year minus the population on 1 January of the current year.
  • Statistical adjustments — the gap between population growth measured that way and the sum of excess of births and net migration. Statistics Norway publishes it as a separate item because delayed reports, annulments and revisions mean the two calculations never agree exactly.

Transformations made for these visualisations

  • Stocks are never mixed with flows on one scale. Population is around 5.6 million; the annual components are in the tens of thousands. They appear in separate views, and the flow diagram deliberately excludes the stock.
  • The heatmap is scaled row by row. Each row is normalised to its own minimum and maximum, because the four series differ by nearly an order of magnitude. Colour therefore shows a year's position within that series, and colours are not comparable between rows. This is stated again on the chart.
  • Statistical adjustments are derived, not read. They are computed as population growth minus (excess of births + net migration), which is Statistics Norway's own definition of the item.
  • No axis is truncated. Bar charts and level lines begin at zero. Where a scale does not begin at zero — the scatter plot — no length is being encoded, so nothing is exaggerated.
  • Missing values are gaps. The flow components for 2026 are not yet published, so 2026 appears in the stock series and not in the flow series. Nothing is interpolated or carried forward.
