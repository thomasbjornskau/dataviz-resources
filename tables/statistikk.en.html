So far we've counted people. Now we measure something.

The first two pages quietly assumed every cell is a count. But a cell can just as well be a sum of kroner, an average, a median. And once the cell measures something, "percent" gains a new and important ambiguity: percent of how many, or percent of how much? We use income and tax, broken down by region.

Part 1

A cell can be more than a count

As long as you only count people, there's really just one statistic: the count. But choose a measure variable — here, income — and each cell can be worked out several ways. The count says how many, the sum says how much altogether, the mean says how much each on average. Switch the statistic and see how the same region tells different things.

Part 3 · the heart of it

Percent of how many, or percent of how much?

This is the new ambiguity. On page 1, percent was always percent of a count. But with a measure variable you can also compute percent of the sum. "What share of the people live in Oslo?" and "what share of the income is earned in Oslo?" are two entirely different questions — and the answers diverge when income per person varies between groups.

The rule of thumb: percent of what?

When a table shows percentages of a measure variable, always ask percent of what — of the number of units, or of the sum? They answer different questions and can give the opposite impression. Oslo has 25% of the people but 32.5% of the income, because income per person is higher there. Nord-Norge is the reverse.

The rule from page 1 still applies on top of this: you also need to know whether the percentage is of the row, the column or the total. "Percent of what" and "percent in which direction" are two independent choices — a well-labelled table answers both.

A note on weighting

When each unit counts differently

In sample surveys, each respondent usually represents several people in the population. Each unit then gets a weight, and the statistics are computed weighted. The point is simple: a weighted mean is pulled toward the heavily weighted units. Here are five people where one counts as five.

The unweighted mean treats all five equally: (300 + 350 + 400 + 450 + 500) / 5 = 400. The weighted one lets the person with weight 5 count correspondingly more: (300 + 350 + 400 + 450×5 + 500×2) / 10 = 430. The difference is the whole point of weighting — the figure is pulled toward the units that represent the most. The count (N) is always unweighted: it's still five people.

In closing

What does the cell measure — and against what?

A table with measure variables demands two questions more than a plain count. First: what is the cell — a count, a sum, a mean? They tell different things about the same group. Then, if it's a percentage: percent of what — of the units or of the sum? Add the direction question from page 1, and you have everything you need to read even a dense statistics table without being misled. Tools like flextab give you all these choices explicitly; the point here is to know which question each figure answers.
