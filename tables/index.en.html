Part 2

Totals are answers, not decoration

A total — what statisticians call a marginal — is the sum along the edge of the table. The row total answers "how many in this row altogether?", the column total does the same going down, and the corner is everyone combined. Switch them on and see where each belongs. Totals are basic table anatomy — they come before we start building the table out.

Part 4 · the heart of it

Three percentages, three different questions — and one is usually right

This is where most people trip up. The same cell can be turned into a percentage three ways, and they don't mean the same thing. But they aren't equal options: which one you should pick depends on what your background variable is. Pick a type below and see which direction the percentage runs.

Percentage type

The rule of thumb: percentage on the background variable

When one variable explains another, you usually compute the percentage within each value of the background variable. Here education is the background and employment is what we want to explain — so we ask "what share are employed within each level of education?". With education as columns that makes it column percent, and each column adds up to 100.

That makes the numbers directly comparable: 53%, 69%, 86% employed — the share rises clearly with education. The opposite percentage answers a different question, and total percent is rarely used — mostly when you want to show what share of the whole a single combination makes up.

In closing

Read the direction before you read the number

When you meet a percentage table: first work out what adds up to 100. That tells you which question the author asked — and whether it's the same question you were wondering about. Then ask whether the percentage was computed on the background variable, as it usually should be. A well-labelled table says this outright. A poorly labelled one lets you guess, and people tend to guess wrong.
